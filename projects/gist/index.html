GIST: What Do VLM Agents Need to Give Good Directions to People?

2026 - In Submission

Team members:
Shivendra Agrawal, Bradley Hayes
RGB and depth from a handheld smartphone in a grocery aisle: dense, long-tail, and geometrically repetitive.

Vision-language models (VLMs) know a lot about what things are, but not where they are. Ask one to guide a person through a grocery store or an office and it will happily describe a shopping cart that has since moved, or tell you to “turn right down the next aisle” without saying which one. GIST asks a simple question: what spatial input does a VLM agent actually need to give a person grounded directions?

GIST builds a lightweight semantic topology from a single scan by a smartphone or a quadruped robot: a traversability graph of walkable space, with open-vocabulary landmarks (products, furniture, signs) bound to its edges in a shared coordinate frame. Geometry (turns, distances, which side a landmark is on) is computed, never generated, and the VLM is only asked to turn those facts into human-centered language.

Offline, one traversal (a phone in a grocery store, a Unitree Go1 in an office) yields an occupancy map, a topology graph, and VLM-annotated landmarks. Online, GIST plans a path on the graph and the VLM turns it into egocentric instructions.

Explicit route facts beat richer visual input

We compared five ways of giving the same VLM a route across 30 routes in two environments: GIST’s route description plus an annotated map image, the route description alone (GIST Text), the map image with only a coarse summary (GIST Visual), a coarse Route Summary, and a future-view image sequence in the style of NavComposer. Two held-out LLM judges from other model families scored the instructions blind on five criteria from spatial-cognition research.

The route description is what matters. With it, adding the map image changes nothing detectable (GIST vs. GIST Text). Without it, the image helps a sparse summary but cannot replace the computed route.

Faithful and fully specified

We audited every GIST and NavComposer instruction against the planned route, blind to condition. GIST instructions were route-consistent on 27 of 30 routes and adequately specified on 29; NavComposer’s on 22 and 16. Two typical instructions show the difference:

Future-view image sequence

"Walk forward past the shopping cart toward the back wall. Turn right into the aisle."

Names a movable object and never says where to turn. On this route the turn comes 3.5 m later, before the last aisle, so a walker heading for the wall can pass it.

In-store pilot. In a preliminary pilot in the grocery store (N=5 sighted participants, 10 trials), participants reached the target in 8 of 10 trials using only the generated verbal instructions. Both failures were bottom-shelf items the chest-height scan never saw. This is early real-world signal, not a study with target users.

Beyond directions

The same topology supports other tasks people need before and during navigation. These are early demonstrations rather than the focus of the paper.

Semantic goal search. A query like "biryani ingredients" is decomposed into items located on the map (left). When nothing matches exactly, as with "vegan protein," the agent suggests mapped alternatives and the zone where such items are likely shelved (right).
Zone classification. Products are grouped into 18 zones and spread over the walkable floor plan, revealing structure such as the store's organic section without it being labeled.

Localization from a single photo. Matching the open-vocabulary description of one image against the topology gives coarse pose proposals, with median errors of 2.69 m (grocery) and 2.42 m (office). Turning this signal into full global localization and tracking is the subject of VLM-GLoc.

    GIST - Multimodal Knowledge Extraction and Spatial Grounding
    Shivendra Agrawal, and Bradley Hayes
    arXiv preprint arXiv:2604.15495, 2026
    Abs Bib PDF

    Navigating complex, densely packed environments like retail stores, warehouses, and hospitals poses a significant spatial grounding challenge for humans and embodied AI. In these spaces, dense visual features quickly become stale given the quasi-static nature of items, and long-tail semantic distributions challenge traditional computer vision. While Vision-Language Models (VLMs) help assistive systems navigate semantically-rich spaces, they still struggle with spatial grounding in cluttered environments. We present GIST (Grounded Intelligent Semantic Topology), a multimodal knowledge extraction pipeline that transforms a consumer-grade mobile point cloud into a semantically annotated navigation topology. Our architecture distills the scene into a 2D occupancy map, extracts its topological layout, and overlays a lightweight semantic layer via intelligent keyframe and semantic selection. We demonstrate the versatility of this structured spatial knowledge through critical downstream Human-AI interaction tasks: (1) an intent-driven Semantic Search engine that actively infers categorical alternatives and zones when exact matches fail; (2) a one-shot Semantic Localizer achieving a 1.04 m top-5 mean translation error; (3) a Zone Classification module that segments the walkable floor plan into high-level semantic regions; and (4) a Visually-Grounded Instruction Generator that synthesizes egocentric, landmark-rich natural language routing. In multi-criteria LLM evaluations, GIST outperforms sequence-based instruction generation baselines. Finally, an in-situ formative evaluation (N=5) yields an 80% navigation success rate relying solely on verbal cues, validating the system’s capacity for universal design.
